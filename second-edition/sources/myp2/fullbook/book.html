How to use this book

Each chapter follows the same sequence, so you always know what is coming next. The sequence is deliberate: you investigate before you are told the rule, because a rule you have discovered is a rule you can defend.

The chapter sequence

  1. Engagement. A short situation that raises the question the chapter will answer.
  2. Inquiry questions. Factual, conceptual and debatable questions that frame the chapter.
  3. Investigation. A task you complete before the formal teaching: record evidence in a table and look for a pattern.
  4. Observation and discovery. What you noticed, stated in your own words and compared with a partner.
  5. Mathematical conclusion. The rule that the evidence supports, written as a general statement.
  6. Formal development. The explanation, definitions and notation, with the reasoning made explicit.
  7. Worked examples. Problem, visual representation, mathematical reasoning, step-by-step solution, final answer and interpretation.
  8. Practice. Separated into Criterion A, B, C and D practice, with visual representations throughout.
  9. Application, assessment and reflection. Real-context tasks, criterion-based assessment practice and a review of your progress.

MYP mathematics and this coursebook

The IB Middle Years Programme asks students to inquire into concepts through global contexts, to develop approaches to learning (ATL) skills, and to act on what they learn through service as action. In mathematics this means more than practising procedures: students investigate patterns, justify generalisations, communicate with precise language and apply mathematics to situations that matter to them.

MYP mathematics is organised into four branches — number, algebra, geometry and trigonometry, and statistics and probability — and the programme recommends at least 50 hours of teaching per subject each year. This coursebook covers the Year 2 content of those branches across eleven chapters, grouped into four parts.

Assessment in MYP mathematics uses four equally weighted criteria, each with a maximum of 8: A knowing and understanding, B investigating patterns, C communicating, and D applying mathematics in real-life contexts. This book trains each criterion explicitly, and says so on the page where the training happens.

Ratios and equivalent ratios

A ratio compares two quantities. The same ratio can describe a snack pack or a factory order, and equivalent ratios keep that comparison honest.

Engage

A school fair sells boxes of 8 pencils and boxes of 12 pencils. A teacher buys 3 boxes of 8 and a parent buys 2 boxes of 12. Who has more pencils? A ratio answers this in one line.

Framing the inquiry

Key concept: Form
Related concepts: Equivalence · Representation
Global context: Fairness and development
Global context exploration: Inequality, difference and inclusion

Statement of inquiry: The form of a ratio, and the equivalence of its different representations, allow a limited resource to be shared fairly and allow that fairness to be justified and checked.

Questions to guide us

  • Factual: What does the ratio 3:5 compare, what is its simplest form, and how do you generate ratios equivalent to it?
  • Conceptual: Why can one ratio describe many different-sized situations, and how does representing it as a unit rate or a ratio table change what you are able to compare?
  • Debatable: Is a ratio always the fairest way to share a limited resource, and how could you use mathematical evidence to defend your position?
ATL · Critical thinking. In order for a student to evaluate and improve a mathematical claim, students must test the claim on several cases, find a counterexample if one exists, and revise the statement so it is accurate.

What do you notice?

Understanding ratio language

A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind. The order matters: 3:5 is not the same comparison as 5:3. A ratio does not tell you the total — it tells you how the total is split.

Two different kinds of comparison are easy to confuse. A part-part ratio compares one part with another (red:blue = 3:5). A part-whole ratio compares one part with everything (red:total = 3:8). Writing the wrong one is the most common error in this chapter.

A ratio can be simplified by dividing both parts by the same non-zero number, exactly as with fractions. 12:18 simplifies to 2:3 because both parts divide by 6.

MYP Mathematics 2 · Student coursebook1.1 · Worked example 1
Chapter 1 · 1.1 · Worked example 1

Understanding ratio language

Worked example 1.1.1

Problem. A fruit bowl holds 12 apples and 18 plums. Write the ratio of apples to plums in simplest form, then write the ratio of apples to all the fruit.

Mathematical reasoning. The ratio compares like quantities, so I can divide both parts by their highest common factor without changing the comparison.

  1. Apples : plums = 12:18.
  2. HCF(12, 18) = 6, so divide both parts by 6.
  3. 12:18 = 2:3.
  4. Total fruit = 12 + 18 = 30, so apples : total = 12:30 = 2:5.

Final answer. apples : plums = 2:3 and apples : total = 2:5

Interpretation. Simplifying did not change the bowl; it only made the comparison easier to read and to scale.

Understanding ratio language

Worked example 1.1.2

Problem. A class has 14 boys and 16 girls. A second class has 21 boys and 24 girls. Do the two classes have the same ratio of boys to girls?

Mathematical reasoning. Two ratios are equivalent when one is a multiple of the other. Scaling 14:16 by 1.5 gives 21:24, so the comparison is the same.

  1. First class: 14:16. Divide both parts by 2 →\rightarrow→ 7:8.
  2. Second class: 21:24. Divide both parts by 3 →\rightarrow→ 7:8.
  3. Both classes reduce to 7:8, so the ratios are equivalent.

Final answer. Yes — both classes have a boys-to-girls ratio of 7:8

Interpretation. The classes are different sizes, but the balance between boys and girls is identical.

What do you notice?

  • The gap between the two parts grows, but the comparison does not change.

Equivalent ratios and simplest form

Equivalent ratios are the ratio version of equivalent fractions. To test whether two ratios are equivalent, simplify both to simplest form, or check whether one multiplier turns one into the other.

A unit rate is a ratio scaled so that one part is 1. It is the most useful form for comparing: 8 pencils for 3 dollars is a unit price of 0.375 dollars per pencil.

Simplifying is also a checking tool. If two answers disagree, reduce both to simplest form before deciding which is wrong.

Equivalent ratios and simplest form

Worked example 1.2.1

Problem. A recipe uses 2 cups of flour for every 3 cups of milk. How much flour is needed for 15 cups of milk?

Mathematical reasoning. The ratio is fixed at 2:3, so milk scaled by 5 means flour also scales by 5.

  1. flour : milk = 2:3.
  2. 15 ÷ 3 = 5, so the milk has been multiplied by 5.
  3. Multiply the flour by the same factor: 2 × 5 = 10.

Final answer. 10 cups of flour

Interpretation. The recipe keeps its taste because both parts moved by the same factor.

Worked example 1.2.2

Problem. Show that 12:16 and 27:36 are equivalent.

Mathematical reasoning. Two ratios are equivalent exactly when their simplest forms are identical.

  1. 12:16: HCF is 4, so 12:16 = 3:4.
  2. 27:36: HCF is 9, so 27:36 = 3:4.
  3. Both simplify to 3:4, so they are equivalent.

Final answer. 12:16 = 27:36 because both equal 3:4

Interpretation. Simplifying first makes the comparison immediate, whatever the sizes.

What do you notice?

  • 25 ÷ 5 = 5 steps, so 5 × 2 = 10 litres.

Ratio tables and double number lines

A ratio table lists matching pairs of quantities. Reading down the table shows one quantity scaling; reading across shows the fixed comparison. A double number line does the same job on a line, which makes missing values visible.

Tables are the natural home for ratio reasoning because they keep the relationship visible while you scale. To find a missing value, first find the multiplier between two known values in the same column.

The same structure appears later as a table of values for a proportional relationship, and as a coordinate graph. Recognising it now saves time in chapters 2 and 6.

Problem. A shade of green uses 3 parts blue to 5 parts yellow. Complete the table and find how much blue is needed for 40 litres of yellow.

Problem. A map scale is 1 cm to 250 m. How many centimetres on the map show 2 km?

Sharing and comparing with ratios

What do you notice?
  • 60:60 simplifies to 1:1, not 5:3.
  • 120 ÷ 8 = 15, so one part is 15 books.
  • 5 × 15 = 75 and 3 × 15 = 45, which adds back to 120.

Chapter 1 · 1.4

Sharing and comparing with ratios

Sharing an amount in a ratio has three steps: add the parts, divide the amount by the total to find the value of one part, then multiply by each part. Skipping the first step is the classic error.

Comparing ratios is easier when both are written with the same second part, or when both are converted to unit rates. Whichever you choose, say which you used.

A ratio also supports reasoning about fairness: if two people contribute in the ratio 3:2, a fair split of the reward is not automatically 3:2 unless the agreed rule says so. State the rule before you calculate.

Sharing and comparing with ratios

Worked example 1.4.1

Problem. Share 56 sweets between two classes in the ratio 4:3.

Mathematical reasoning. The value of one part is the total divided by the number of parts.

  1. Total parts: 4 + 3 = 7.
  2. One part: 56 ÷ 7 = 8 sweets.
  3. First class: 4 × 8 = 32. Second class: 3 × 8 = 24.
  4. Check: 32 + 24 = 56.

Final answer. 32 sweets and 24 sweets

Interpretation. The check is part of the answer: the parts must add back to the total.

Worked example 1.4.2

Problem. Box A holds 15 red and 10 blue counters. Box B holds 21 red and 14 blue. Which box has the greater proportion of red counters?

Mathematical reasoning. Compare the same part in both ratios: red to total, or red to blue.

  1. Box A: 15:10 = 3:2, so red is 3 of 5 parts = 60%.
  2. Box B: 21:14 = 3:2, so red is also 60%.

Recognising proportional relationships

Two quantities are in a proportional relationship when one is always a constant multiple of the other. That constant is the constant of proportionality, often written k, and y = kx.

A quick test: divide each pair of matching values. If every quotient is the same, the relationship is proportional. If even one quotient differs, it is not.

Proportional relationships are a special case of linear relationships. Every proportional relationship is linear, but a linear relationship with a non-zero starting value is not proportional.

The unit rate is the constant of proportionality when the second quantity is 1. Comparing unit rates is the most reliable way to decide which of two offers is better value.

Recognising proportional relationships

Worked example 2.1.1

Problem. A taxi charges 3.50 dollars per kilometre with no fixed fee. Is the cost proportional to distance? What is k?

Mathematical reasoning. There is no fixed starting fee, so cost = k × distance with k = 3.50.

  1. Divide cost by distance for each row: 3.50, 3.50, 3.50, 3.50.
  2. Every quotient is the same, so the relationship is proportional.
  3. k = 3.50, so cost = 3.50 × distance.

Final answer. Yes, proportional with k = 3.50 dollars per kilometre

Interpretation. Because k is constant, doubling the distance exactly doubles the cost.

Recognising proportional relationships

Worked example 2.1.2

Problem. A plumber charges a 40-dollar call-out fee plus 25 dollars per hour. Is the charge proportional to hours worked?

Mathematical reasoning. A proportional relationship must have zero charge at zero hours. Here one hour already costs 65 dollars.

  1. At 0 hours the charge is still 40 dollars, not 0.
  2. The quotients are 65, 45, 38.3, 35 — not constant.
  3. So the relationship is linear but not proportional.

Final answer. No — there is a fixed starting value of 40 dollars

Interpretation. A graph of this relationship is a straight line that does not pass through the origin.

Unit rates and comparison

A unit rate scales a ratio so the second quantity is 1: dollars per item, kilometres per hour, grams per dollar. Unit rates make unlike quantities comparable.

To compare two offers, convert both to the same unit rate. Whichever is smaller per unit is better value when you are paying; whichever is larger is better when you are earning.

Unit rates also let you convert units cleanly: 60 km/h is 1 km per minute, because 60 ÷ 60 = 1.

Worked example 2.2.1

Problem. Shop A sells 5 kg of rice for 12 dollars. Shop B sells 8 kg for 18 dollars. Which is better value?

Mathematical reasoning. Convert both to cost per kilogram, then compare.

  1. Shop A: 12 ÷ 5 = 2.40 dollars per kg.

Worked example 2.2.2

Problem. A cyclist travels 45 km in 3 hours. Find the speed in km/h and the distance covered in 20 minutes.

Chapter 2 · 2.3

Graphs of proportional relationships

A proportional relationship graphs as a straight line through the origin. The slope of the line is the constant of proportionality.

Reading a value off the graph is a form of interpolation; reading beyond the data is extrapolation. Both are valid when the relationship genuinely stays proportional, and both need a note about that assumption.

The graph also shows what the table hides: the steepness of the line tells you at a glance which relationship grows faster.

Worked example 2.3.1

Problem. A machine fills 60 bottles per minute. Draw the graph of bottles against time and read off the number filled in 7 minutes.

Mathematical reasoning. Bottles = 60 × time, so the graph is a line through the origin with slope 60.

  1. Plot the points (1, 60), (2, 120), (5, 300) and (10, 600).
  2. Join them with a straight line through (0, 0).
  3. At 7 minutes the line reaches 7 × 60 = 420.

Graphs of proportional relationships

Worked example 2.3.2

Problem. Two printers print at 12 and 18 pages per minute. Sketch both lines and state which is steeper and why.

Mathematical reasoning. Both lines pass through the origin; the steeper line has the larger constant.

  1. P: pages = 12 × time. Q: pages = 18 × time.

  3. Q's line is steeper because 18 > 12.

Final answer. Printer Q, with a constant of 18 pages per minute

Interpretation. On a graph, the constant of proportionality is the steepness of the line.

Scaling and real-world models

Scaling multiplies every length by the same factor. Areas scale by the square of that factor and volumes by its cube — a fact that surprises most learners the first time.

A scale drawing keeps angles unchanged and multiplies every length by the scale factor. A scale model is a three-dimensional version of the same idea.

Percentage change is a proportional relationship with k = the percentage written as a decimal. A 15% increase multiplies by 1.15.

Worked example 2.4.1

Problem. A rectangle is 6 cm by 4 cm. It is enlarged by scale factor 3. Find the new dimensions and the new area.

Mathematical reasoning. Lengths scale by 3, so the area scales by 3² = 9.

    Understanding rates

    A rate compares quantities of different kinds, so it always carries two units: kilometres per hour, dollars per kilogram, pages per minute. A ratio compares quantities of the same kind.

    Speed, price per unit and flow are the three rates learners meet most often. Each is a constant of proportionality between its two quantities.

    Average speed is total distance divided by total time, not the average of the speeds. Confusing the two is the most common error in rate problems.

    Converting units

    Conversion is proportional reasoning with a known constant. 1 km = 1 000 m means every kilometre is 1 000 metres, so distance in metres = 1 000 × distance in kilometres.

    To convert a compound rate, convert one unit at a time and keep the fraction structure. 60 km/h becomes 60 000 m/h, then 1 000 m/min, then about 16.7 m/s.

    Choosing the order of conversion matters for accuracy: converting to the smaller unit first avoids repeating decimals.

    Density and pressure

    Density is mass per unit volume: density = mass ÷ volume. Two objects with the same volume can have very different masses, which is what density measures.

    Pressure is force per unit area: pressure = force ÷ area. A smaller area with the same force gives a larger pressure, which is why a knife is sharp.

    Both are rates, so both use the same reasoning: fix the denominator to 1 and compare.

      Chapter 4 · 4.1

      The integer number line

      Integers are the whole numbers together with their negatives: … −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3 … The number line gives them an order: a number further right is always greater.

      Zero is the reference point. A positive number lies to the right of zero, a negative number to the left. The sign gives the direction and the digit gives the distance from zero.

      Opposites are pairs like +5 and −5. They sit the same distance from zero on opposite sides, and they add to zero.

      Inequality signs can be read directly from the line: −7 < −2 because −7 lies further left.

      Adding and subtracting integers

      Addition is a move along the line: adding a positive moves right, adding a negative moves left. Subtraction is the same move in the opposite direction.

      The two rules follow from this model. Adding a negative is the same as subtracting the positive: 5 + (−3) = 5 − 3 = 2. Subtracting a negative is the same as adding the positive: 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8.

      A written calculation can always be checked on a number line. If the move and the answer disagree, the mistake is in the arithmetic, not in the model.

        Simplifying and expanding

        Like terms have the same variables raised to the same powers. 3x and 5x are like terms; 3x and 5x² are not. Only like terms can be collected.

        Simplifying collects like terms: 3x + 5x = 8x. The coefficients add; the variable part is unchanged.

        Expanding multiplies each term inside a bracket by the term outside: 3(x + 4) = 3x + 12. Expanding and collecting are opposite processes, and both are checked by substituting a value.

        Solving equations

        An equation states that two expressions are equal. Solving it means finding the value that makes the statement true.

        The balance method keeps the equation true by doing the same thing to both sides: add, subtract, multiply or divide by the same amount.

        Always check a solution by substituting it back into the original equation. A solution that fails the check is a signal that a sign or a step went wrong.

          Describing sequences

          A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. A term is one entry; its position is usually called n. The nth term rule gives the value at position n.

          To find a rule, look at the differences between consecutive terms. Constant differences mean a linear rule; constant second differences mean a quadratic rule.

          A rule is a generalisation: it claims to work for every position. Testing one new term is evidence, and checking the structure is proof.

              Naming and drawing solids

              A solid has faces, edges and vertices. A cuboid has 6 faces, 12 edges and 8 vertices; a cube is a cuboid with all edges equal.

              Chapter 8 · 8.2

              Volume of prisms and cylinders

              Volume measures the space inside a solid and is measured in cubic units. A cuboid of length l, width w and height h has volume lwh.

              Any prism has volume equal to the area of its cross-section times its length. This single rule covers cuboids, triangular prisms and cylinders.

              A cylinder has volume πr²h, because its cross-section is a circle. The radius and the height must use the same unit.

              One cubic centimetre is one millilitre, and 1 000 cm³ is one litre. Volume conversions use powers of ten, exactly like length.

              Surface area of solids

              Surface area is the total area of every face. For a cuboid it is 2(lw + lh + wh), and for a cube of side s it is 6s².

              A cylinder has surface area 2πr² + 2πrh: two circles plus a rectangle that wraps around. The rectangle has height h and width equal to the circumference 2πr.

              Surface area problems in packaging ask for the least material, which means finding the shape with the smallest surface area for a fixed volume.

              Nets are the tool for irregular solids: draw the net, find each face's area, then add.

                  Describing probability

                  The probability of an event is a number from 0 to 1. Zero means impossible, 1 means certain, and 0.5 means equally likely to happen or not.

                  Theoretical probability comes from counting equally likely outcomes: P(event) = number of favourable outcomes ÷ total number of outcomes. Experimental probability comes from repeating a trial.

                  Probabilities of all outcomes of one experiment add to 1, so the probability of 'not A' is 1 − P(A).

                  Probability can be written as a fraction, a decimal or a percentage, but the scale from 0 to 1 is the reference for all of them.

                  Probability rules

                  For mutually exclusive events, P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B). If they are not mutually exclusive, the overlap must be subtracted.

                  For independent events, P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Dependence means the first event changes the probability of the second.

                  These two rules cover most probability calculations, and choosing between them depends on whether the question says 'or' or 'and'.

                  A tree diagram makes the structure visible: add along the branches for 'or', multiply along a path for 'and'.

                  Simulation and fair games

                  A simulation uses random numbers to model a situation that is impractical to repeat. A random number table, dice, coins or a calculator can all generate the outcomes.

                  A fair game gives each player the same expected chance of winning. Expected value is the average result over many repetitions, and it decides whether a game is worth playing.

                    Measures of centre

                    The mean is the sum of the values divided by how many there are. It uses every value, so it is affected by extreme values.

                    The median is the middle value when the data are ordered. With an even number of values it is the mean of the two middle values, and it is unaffected by extremes.

                    The mode is the most frequent value. It is the only average that works for categorical data.

                    Choosing an average is a decision: the mean for a balanced summary, the median when there are outliers, the mode for categories.

                    What you should now be able to do

                    Key ideas

                    • A display must match the data type and the question.
                    • The mean uses every value; the median is unaffected by extremes; the mode suits categories.
                    • The range and the IQR measure spread.
                    • A box plot shows minimum, Q1, median, Q3 and maximum together.
                    • Comparing data sets needs both centre and spread.
                    • A critical reader asks which average, which sample and what spread.
