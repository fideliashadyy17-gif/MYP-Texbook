Powers, expanded form and order of operations

A power is a compressed instruction: it records how many copies of a factor to multiply. A shared order of operations keeps that compression unambiguous.

Engage

A storage label says “10 TB”. A computer reports the same space as “about 9.09 TiB”. Has storage disappeared? And when someone writes [[math:2+3\times4]], what must we agree before the expression has one meaning?

Framing the inquiry

Key concept: Form
Related concepts: Simplification, Quantity
Global context: Scientific and technical innovation

Statement of inquiry: Compressing repeated multiplication into a power, and agreeing on the order in which operations apply, allows complex calculations to be recorded compactly and reproduced reliably.

Questions to guide us

  • Factual: What does [[math:5^3]] mean? Which operation comes first in [[math:6+2\times3]]?
  • Conceptual: Why does an exponent of 1 leave the base unchanged? How can [[math:2^{10}]] and [[math:10^3]] be compared fairly?
  • Debatable: Should a calculator decide the meaning of an expression, or should the writer make the meaning visible?
ATL · Transfer. In order for a student to transfer a rule to a new situation, students must check whether the structure is the same, apply the rule carefully, and test the result with an example or counterexample.
Getting ready

Learning intentions and prerequisite check

You are learning to

  • Understand a power as repeated multiplication and identify its base and exponent.
  • Use powers of ten in expanded form and return to standard form.
  • Recognise squares and cubes, and find square roots and cube roots.
  • Apply the order of operations, including brackets, indices and roots.
  • Substitute values into expressions and formulas containing powers.
  • Compare quantities written as powers and justify the comparison.

Success criteria

  • I can expand and evaluate a power without reading the exponent as a multiplier.
  • I can explain why [[math:a^1=a]] and, for [[math:a\neq0]], [[math:a^0=1]].
  • I can move between standard form and expanded form with powers of ten.
  • I can reverse squaring and cubing with roots.
  • I can show one operation per line and respect brackets.
  • I can explain how brackets change the base of a power.

2.1 · From repeated multiplication to powers

The exponent counts factors

Writing [[math:3\times3\times3\times3]] is correct but long. Index notation records two things: the repeated factor, called the base, and the number of copies, called the exponent or index.

{{figure:power-anatomy|In 3⁴, the base 3 is repeated four times. The value 81 is the result, not part of the notation.}}
Worked example 1

Write each power as repeated multiplication and evaluate it: [[math:2^5]], [[math:5^2]], [[math:10^4]], [[math:4^3]].

  1. [[math:2^5=2\times2\times2\times2\times2=32]].
  2. [[math:5^2=5\times5=25]]. This is a square.
  3. [[math:10^4=10\times10\times10\times10=10{,}000]].
  4. [[math:4^3=4\times4\times4=64]]. This is a cube.

Check: the number of factors must equal the exponent.

2.1 · From repeated multiplication to powers

Special cases and common errors

An exponent of 1 means one factor: [[math:7^1=7]]. For a nonzero base, an exponent of 0 is defined to be 1. The pattern of dividing by the base explains the convention:

[[math:5^3=125,\quad 5^2=25,\quad 5^1=5,\quad 5^0=1]].

Misconception check

[[math:3^4\neq3\times4]]. The exponent tells how many times to use the base as a factor. Likewise, [[math:5^2=25]], not 10.

Worked example 2

Write each product as a power and evaluate it: [[math:6\times6\times6\times6]], [[math:9\times9]], [[math:2\times2\times2\times2\times2\times2\times2]].

  1. Four factors of 6: [[math:6^4=1{,}296]].
  2. Two factors of 9: [[math:9^2=81]].
  3. Seven factors of 2: [[math:2^7=128]].

Count the factors before writing the exponent.

2.2 · Powers of ten and expanded form

Place value is built from powers of ten

The ones place is [[math:10^0]], the tens place is [[math:10^1]], the hundreds place is [[math:10^2]], and so on. Each step left multiplies by 10; each step right divides by 10.

{{figure:powers-of-ten-chart|Place values from millions to ones written as powers of ten.}}
Worked example 3

Write 5,207 in expanded form using powers of ten.

  1. Read the places: 5 thousands, 2 hundreds, 0 tens, 7 ones.
  2. Replace each place by a power of ten: [[math:5\times10^3+2\times10^2+0\times10^1+7\times10^0]].
  3. Check: [[math:5{,}000+200+0+7=5{,}207]].

The zero term contributes nothing but preserves the place structure.

{{exercise:p22:2.2.1,2.2.2,2.2.3}}
2.2 · Powers of ten and expanded form

Return to standard form without losing zeros

Expanded form is longer than standard form because it exposes the value of every digit. That makes it useful for checking place value and for seeing the effect of multiplying or dividing by 10.

Worked example 4

Write [[math:6\times10^4+0\times10^3+3\times10^2+9\times10^1+0\times10^0]] in standard form. State the value of the digit 3.

  1. Evaluate nonzero terms: [[math:60{,}000]], [[math:300]], [[math:90]].
  2. Keep the empty thousands and ones places: 60,390.
  3. The 3 is in the hundreds place, so it is worth 300.

60,390; the digit 3 contributes 300.

Misconception check

[[math:10^4]] is not 40. It is [[math:10\times10\times10\times10=10{,}000]].

{{exercise:p22:2.2.4,2.2.5,2.2.6,2.2.7,2.2.8}}
2.3 · Squares, cubes and roots

Models for squares and cubes

A square number is a whole number multiplied by itself. The positive square numbers begin 1, 4, 9, 16, … A cube number is a whole number multiplied by itself twice: 1, 8, 27, 64, …

{{figure:square-cube-models|A square shows two equal dimensions; a cube shows three. The cube can be seen as 4 layers of 16 unit cubes.}}
Worked example 5

Evaluate [[math:12^2]], [[math:7^3]], [[math:\sqrt{81}]], [[math:\sqrt{144}]] and [[math:\sqrt[3]{64}]].

  1. [[math:12^2=144]]; [[math:7^3=343]].
  2. [[math:9^2=81]], so [[math:\sqrt{81}=9]].
  3. [[math:12^2=144]], so [[math:\sqrt{144}=12]].
  4. [[math:4^3=64]], so [[math:\sqrt[3]{64}=4]].

Reverse a root by squaring or cubing the result.

2.3 · Squares, cubes and roots

Roots reverse powers; they do not split sums

The square root symbol asks for the nonnegative value whose square is the number under the root. The cube root asks for the value whose cube is the number. In geometry, roots recover a side or edge from an area or volume.

Worked example 6

A square has area 121 cm². A cube has volume 125 cm³. Find the side or edge. If the cube’s edge doubles, what happens to its volume?

  1. [[math:\sqrt{121}=11]], so the square’s side is 11 cm.
  2. [[math:\sqrt[3]{125}=5]], so the cube’s edge is 5 cm.
  3. Doubling all three dimensions multiplies volume by [[math:2^3=8]].

The new cube volume is 8 times the original.

Misconception check

[[math:\sqrt{36+64}=\sqrt{100}=10]], but [[math:\sqrt{36}+\sqrt{64}=6+8=14]]. A root applies to the whole value underneath it.

{{exercise:p23:2.3.4,2.3.5,2.3.6,2.3.7,2.3.8}}
2.4 · Order of operations

A shared convention makes expressions readable

Without an agreed order, [[math:2+3\times4]] could mean 20 or 14. Mathematics uses a convention so that the same expression has the same value for every reader.

PriorityOperationsHow to apply it
1Brackets and grouping symbolsWork from the innermost bracket outwards. A fraction bar groups the numerator and denominator.
2Indices and rootsEvaluate powers and roots before multiplication, division, addition or subtraction.
3Multiplication and divisionSame priority: work left to right.
4Addition and subtractionSame priority: work left to right.
Worked example 7

Evaluate [[math:7+2\times3^2]], [[math:(7+2)\times3^2]], [[math:24\div4\times2]] and [[math:24\div(4\times2)]].

  1. [[math:7+2\times9=7+18=25]].
  2. [[math:9\times9=81]].
  3. Left to right: [[math:24\div4=6]], then [[math:6\times2=12]].
  4. Brackets first: [[math:24\div8=3]].

25, 81, 12, 3.

{{exercise:p24:2.4.1,2.4.2,2.4.3}}
2.4 · Order of operations

Brackets, fraction bars and the unary minus

Brackets are the writer’s tool for changing the order. A fraction bar acts like brackets around the whole numerator and the whole denominator.

{{figure:order-of-operations-flow|Evaluate the power first, then multiply, then add. Brackets could change this order.}}
Worked example 8

Evaluate [[math:(18-4\times2)^2\div5]] and [[math:\frac{36-6}{2+4}]].

  1. Inside the bracket: [[math:4\times2=8]], so [[math:18-8=10]]. Then [[math:10^2\div5=100\div5=20]].
  2. Numerator: [[math:36-6=30]]. Denominator: [[math:2+4=6]]. Then [[math:30\div6=5]].

20 and 5.

Boundary note

A minus sign in front of a power is not part of the base unless brackets include it: [[math:-3^2=-(3^2)=-9]], while [[math:(-3)^2=9]].

2.4 · Order of operations

Diagnose the error, then repair the calculation

When a calculation goes wrong, identify the first line where the convention was broken. Do not only change the final answer.

Incorrect

[[math:12+6\div3=18\div3=6]]

The student added before dividing.

Correct

[[math:12+6\div3=12+2=14]]

Division has higher priority than addition.

Communicate

Write one operation per line. If a reader cannot see which rule you used, add a bracket or a short label rather than more words.

2.5 · Substitution and expressions with powers

Substitute first, then ask what is squared

A letter stands for a number. Substitution means replacing the letter with its value and evaluating using the order of operations. Keep brackets around the replacement so the structure stays visible.

{{figure:brackets-change-base|The brackets determine whether only x is squared or the whole product 3x is squared.}}
Worked example 9

Evaluate [[math:3x^2]] and [[math:(3x)^2]] when [[math:x=4]].

  1. [[math:3x^2=3\times4^2=3\times16=48]].
  2. [[math:(3x)^2=(3\times4)^2=12^2=144]].
  3. The second expression squares the whole product, so it is three times the first.

48 and 144.

{{exercise:guided:G3}} {{exercise:p25:2.5.1,2.5.2,2.5.3}}
2.5 · Substitution and expressions with powers

Formulas connect powers to measurement

Area and volume formulas use powers because they multiply equal dimensions. A model may use a power to describe growth or motion; always check what the variables and units mean.

Worked example 10

Find the area of a square with [[math:s=7]] cm, the volume of a cube with [[math:s=4]] cm, and the model distance [[math:d=5t^2]] when [[math:t=3]].

  1. [[math:A=s^2=7^2=49\text{ cm}^2]].
  2. [[math:V=s^3=4^3=64\text{ cm}^3]].
  3. [[math:d=5\times3^2=5\times9=45\text{ m}]]. The index is evaluated before multiplying by 5.

49 cm², 64 cm³, 45 m. The distance formula is a simplified model.

{{exercise:p25:2.5.4,2.5.5,2.5.6}}
Reasoning habit

Before calculating, say what the exponent applies to: a single variable, a product, or a sum.

2.5 · Substitution and expressions with powers

Test a transferred rule

A rule that works in one structure may fail in another. Test the claim [[math:(a+b)^2=a^2+b^2]] before using it.

Worked example 11

Test the claim with [[math:a=3,b=4]], then with [[math:a=2,b=5]].

  1. [[math:(3+4)^2=49]], but [[math:3^2+4^2=25]].
  2. [[math:(2+5)^2=49]], but [[math:2^2+5^2=29]].
  3. One counterexample is enough to reject a universal claim.

The claim is false. Brackets change the base of the power.

{{exercise:p25:2.5.7,2.5.8}}

Common slips

  • Reading an exponent as a multiplier: [[math:5^2\neq10]].
  • Forgetting that [[math:a^0=1]] only for nonzero [[math:a]].
  • Doing multiplication before division just because it appears first in a mnemonic.
  • Squaring only part of a bracketed product or sum.

  • Name the place before writing a power-of-ten term.

  • Square roots and cube roots reverse squaring and cubing.
