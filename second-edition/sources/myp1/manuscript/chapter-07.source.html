Chapter 7 · Mathematical development

7.1 Lines, angles and angle facts

Big idea

Name the relationship first: straight line, full turn, vertically opposite or right angle.

Angles on a straight line add to 180°; angles around a point add to 360°. Vertically opposite angles are equal. A right angle is 90°.

Classify angles as acute (<90°), right (90°), obtuse (between 90° and 180°) or reflex (between 180° and 360°). A diagram is not always drawn to scale, so use stated facts rather than appearance.

{{figure:crossing_lines-7|Vertically opposite angles are equal; adjacent angles on a straight line sum to 180°.}}
Worked example · Find a missing angle

Two lines cross. One angle is 115°.

  1. The vertically opposite angle is 115°.
  2. Each adjacent angle is 180°−115° = 65°.
115°, 65°, 115°, 65° around the intersection.

Chapter 7 · Mathematical development

7.2 Parallel lines and triangles

Big idea

Use corresponding, alternate and co-interior angles only when the lines are parallel.

When a transversal crosses parallel lines, corresponding angles are equal, alternate angles are equal, and co-interior angles add to 180°.

Triangle angles sum to 180°. An exterior angle equals the sum of the two opposite interior angles. In an isosceles triangle, the angles opposite the equal sides are equal.

{{figure:parallel_lines-7|With parallel lines: corresponding and alternate angles are 65°; the co-interior angle is 115°.}}
Worked example · A roof truss

The two base angles are 65° each.

  1. Apex = 180−65−65 = 50°.
  2. The exterior angle at a base is 180−65 = 115°.
Apex 50°; exterior base angle 115°.

Chapter 7 · Mathematical development

7.3 Polygons and the triangle inequality

Big idea

Split polygons into triangles; test whether three lengths can close a triangle.

An n-sided polygon can be split into n−2 triangles from one vertex, so its interior angle sum is (n−2)×180°. The exterior angles of any convex polygon sum to 360°.

For a regular n-gon, each exterior angle is 360°/n and each interior angle is 180°−360°/n. Three lengths form a non-degenerate triangle exactly when the sum of the two shorter lengths exceeds the longest.

{{figure:polygon-7|A hexagon splits into four triangles: interior sum 4 × 180° = 720°.}}
Worked example · A pentagon and a possible triangle

Find the interior sum of a pentagon; test sides 3, 4, 8.

  1. Pentagon: (5−2)×180 = 540°.
  2. 3+4 = 7 < 8, so no triangle.
540°; no triangle.

Chapter 7 · Mathematical development

7.4 Ruler-and-compass constructions

Big idea

Use equal radii to force a property, keep construction arcs visible and check the defining relationship.

A perpendicular bisector cuts a segment at its midpoint at 90°. Draw equal-radius arcs from both endpoints, with radius greater than half the segment, then join the arc intersections.

An angle bisector uses an arc from the vertex, then equal arcs from the two arm intersections. For an SSS triangle, draw one side and use the other two lengths as arc radii from its endpoints.

{{figure:compass_bisector-7|Equal radii from A and B locate P and Q. Line PQ is the perpendicular bisector of AB.}}
Worked example · Construct a 5–6–7 triangle

Use the longest side as the base.

  1. Draw AB = 7 cm.
  2. From A draw radius 5 cm; from B draw radius 6 cm. Let the arcs meet at C.
  3. Join AC and BC; check 5+6>7.
Triangle ABC has sides 5, 6 and 7 cm.
