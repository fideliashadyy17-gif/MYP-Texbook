Chapter 5 · Opener

Percentages and applications

Percentages express change and comparison relative to a whole; identifying that whole makes financial and fairness decisions more reliable.

  • Find percentages and percentage changes.

Chapter 5 · Mathematical development

5.1 Per cent and equivalent representations

Big idea

Read “per cent” as “out of one hundred”, then connect fractions, decimals and percentages.

A percentage is a ratio with denominator 100. 37% means 37/100 = 0.37. A percentage can exceed 100 when the amount is more than the reference whole.

To write a fraction as a percentage, find an equivalent fraction out of 100 or multiply by 100%. To write a percentage as a fraction, put it over 100 and simplify.

{{figure:percent_grid-5|37 of 100 equal squares: 37/100 = 0.37 = 37%.}}
Worked example · Connect 3/5, 0.6 and 60%

Scale the fraction to hundredths.

  1. 3/5 = 60/100.
  2. 60/100 = 0.60 = 60%.
All three name the same proportion.

Chapter 5 · Mathematical development

5.2 Finding a percentage of a quantity

Big idea

Translate “of” into multiplication and identify the reference amount first.

p% of A means p/100 × A. Useful benchmarks are 10% (divide by 10), 1% (divide by 100), 50% (halve) and 25% (quarter). Build other percentages from these.

For 15% of 240, find 10% = 24 and 5% = 12, then add: 36. Or use the multiplier 0.15 × 240 = 36.

Worked example · Find 12.5% of 320

Use a fraction benchmark.

  1. 12.5% = 1/8.
  2. 320 ÷ 8 = 40.
40.

Chapter 5 · Mathematical development

5.3 Percentage increase and decrease

Big idea

Find the change from the original amount, or use a single multiplier.

An increase of p% uses multiplier 1 + p/100; a decrease uses 1 − p/100. The original amount is the reference unless the question says otherwise.

Percentage change = change/original × 100%. A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease uses 1.2 × 0.8 = 0.96, ending at 96% of the start.

{{figure:multiplier_flow-5|Each multiplier acts on the current amount: 250 → 200 → 180.}}
Worked example · £250 with 20% off, then 10% off

Apply each multiplier to the current price.

  1. 250 × 0.8 = 200.
  2. 200 × 0.9 = 180.
  3. A single 30% discount would give 175.
£180; total discount 28%, not 30%.

Chapter 5 · Mathematical development

5.4 Discounts, taxes, profit and budgets

Big idea

Separate revenue, costs and profit; apply percentages to the correct line of a budget.

A discount reduces a marked price. A tax or tip usually increases a stated base. Revenue is money received; cost is money spent; profit is revenue minus all relevant costs.

For a budget, calculate each line, then add. Whole-item purchases may require rounding up. A lower unit price is not automatically a better decision if extra stock cannot be sold.

Worked example · A market-stall budget

Sell 20 honey jars at £8.10, 36 soaps at £3 and 25 jams at £5.

  1. Revenue = 162 + 108 + 125 = £395.
  2. Goods cost = 20×6 + 36×2.50 + 25×3.20 = £290.
  3. Fixed costs = 45 + 18 = £63.
Net profit = 395 − 290 − 63 = £42.
