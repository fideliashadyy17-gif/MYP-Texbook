Chapter 8 · Mathematical development

8.1 Sets, elements and subsets

Big idea

Define a set clearly, list each element once and distinguish “element of” from “subset of”.

A set is a well-defined collection. x [[math:\in]] A means x is an element of A; B [[math:\subseteq]] A means every element of B is also in A. Order and repeated listings do not change a set.

The empty set [[math:\emptyset]] has no elements and is a subset of every set. A set with n distinct elements has 2ⁿ subsets, because each element is either included or excluded.

{{figure:subset_tree-8|Each element has two choices: in or out. Three elements give 2³ = 8 subsets.}}
Worked example · Subsets of {a,b,c}

Choose included or excluded for each element.

  1. 2×2×2 = 8 subsets.
  2. [[math:\emptyset]], {a}, {b}, {c}, {a,b}, {a,c}, {b,c}, {a,b,c}.
8 subsets.

8.2 Venn diagrams and set operations

Big idea

Place the overlap first, then the “only” regions, then the outside region.

Union A [[math:\cup]] B contains elements in A or B or both. Intersection A [[math:\cap]] B contains elements in both. Complement A′ contains elements in the universal set [[math:\xi]] that are not in A.

For finite sets, n(A[[math:\cup]]B)=n(A)+n(B)−n(A[[math:\cap]]B). Subtract the overlap once because it was counted in both totals.

{{figure:venn-8|Overlap first: 11 + 6 + 8 + 5 = 30.}}
Worked example · Complete a Venn diagram

[[math:\xi]] has 30 students; 17 help at a fair, 14 at a concert, 6 at both.

  1. Both: 6. Fair only: 17−6=11. Concert only: 14−6=8.
  2. At least one: 11+6+8=25. Neither: 30−25=5.
Regions 11, 6, 8, 5.

Chapter 8 · Mathematical development

8.3 Number sets and the real line

Big idea

Classify a number by the sets it belongs to, not by the way it is written.

Natural numbers [[math:\mathbb{N}]] are counting numbers (here 1,2,3,…); whole numbers add 0; integers [[math:\mathbb{Z}]] add negatives; rationals [[math:\mathbb{Q}]] are ratios of integers with nonzero denominator. Real numbers [[math:\mathbb{R}]] include rationals and irrationals.

Thus natural numbers are a subset of whole numbers, which are a subset of integers, which are a subset of rationals, which are a subset of reals. A terminating or repeating decimal is rational. Non-repeating, non-terminating decimals such as √2 and π are irrational.

{{figure:number_sets-8|Natural numbers sit inside whole numbers, integers, rationals and reals. Irrationals are real but not rational.}}
Worked example · Classify −3, 0, 0.75 and √2

Use the most specific familiar set, then note wider membership.

  1. −3 is an integer and rational.
  2. 0 is whole, integer and rational.
  3. 0.75 = 3/4 is rational.
  4. √2 is irrational and real.
All four are real; only √2 is irrational.

8.4 Sets in surveys and decisions

Big idea

Define the universal set and categories before counting; state whether “or” includes overlap.

A survey count is only meaningful with its universal set. “Pool users” may include people who also use the gym; “pool only” excludes them.

Use a Venn diagram or table to organise counts, check that regions sum to the total, and translate the result into a decision with a stated assumption.

Worked example · A centre audit

80 members: 44 pool, 37 gym, 18 both.

  1. Pool only 26; gym only 19; both 18; neither 17.
  2. At least one: 63, which is 78.75% of 80.
Regions 26, 18, 19, 17; 78.75% use at least one.
