01

Number systems and place value

Ten digits are enough to write any number we can name. Their power comes from position: the same symbol can mean seven, seventy or seven thousandths.

Engage

A museum label says “about 49,000 objects”, while an inventory says “48,672 objects”. Which number would you put on a poster? Which would you use to check the collection? What changes when we round?

Framing the inquiry

Key concept: Form
Related concepts: Quantity, Representation
Global context: Orientation in space and time

Statement of inquiry: The position of a digit in a base-ten system encodes its value, so a small set of symbols can represent any magnitude when place and zero are used consistently.

Questions to guide us

  • Factual: What is the value of a digit in a given position?
  • Conceptual: Why is zero essential, and how can one number have several equivalent representations?
  • Debatable: When is “about 2,000” more useful than the exact count?
ATL · Critical thinking. In order for a student to evaluate and improve a mathematical claim, students must test the claim on several cases, find a counterexample if one exists, and revise the statement so it is accurate.
Getting ready

Learning intentions and prerequisite check

You are learning to

  • Understand how natural numbers, whole numbers, integers and rational numbers are related.
  • Know that the value of a digit depends on its place in a base-ten number.
  • Write whole numbers and decimals in expanded form and return to standard form.
  • Compare and order integers and decimals using place value and a number line.
  • Round to a stated place and choose a reasonable level of accuracy in context.
  • Estimate a result and explain whether the estimate is likely to be too high or too low.

Success criteria

  • I can classify a number and explain the role of zero.
  • I can state the value of any digit in a whole number or decimal.
  • I can move between standard form, expanded form and a number line.
  • I can order positive and negative numbers and justify the order.
  • I can round to a stated place and avoid an over-precise answer.
  • I can estimate, check exactly, and explain the difference.

1.1 · Number systems and the number line

Counting numbers, zero and negatives

Natural numbers are the counting numbers [[math:1,2,3,\ldots]]. In this book, whole numbers are the natural numbers together with zero: [[math:0,1,2,3,\ldots]]. Integers include the whole numbers and their negatives: [[math:\ldots,-3,-2,-1,0,1,2,3,\ldots]].

A number line gives every number a position. Values increase from left to right. Zero is the boundary between negative and positive numbers; it is neither positive nor negative.

{{figure:number-line-integers|Values increase from left to right. The point 2.5 has a precise position even though it is not an integer.}}
Worked example 1

Classify [[math:-4]], [[math:0]] and [[math:12]].

  1. [[math:-4]] is an integer and a rational number, but not whole or natural.
  2. [[math:0]] is whole, an integer and rational, but not natural in this book.
  3. [[math:12]] is natural, whole, an integer and rational.

Each number can belong to more than one family.

1.1 · Number systems and the number line

Rational numbers and number families

A rational number can be written as a fraction [[math:\frac{a}{b}]] where [[math:a]] and [[math:b]] are integers and [[math:b\neq 0]]. Terminating decimals such as [[math:1.2]] and fractions such as [[math:\frac{5}{8}]] are rational. Every integer is rational because, for example, [[math:-4=\frac{-4}{1}]].

{{figure:number-families|Natural numbers sit inside whole numbers; whole numbers sit inside integers; integers sit inside rational numbers, which also include fractions and terminating decimals.}}
Misconception check

“Zero is not a number because it means nothing.” Zero is a number with a precise position. It also holds a place: [[math:305]] is not the same as [[math:35]].

Worked example 2

Order [[math:-7,-2,0,3,5]] from least to greatest.

  1. On a number line, more negative values lie further left.
  2. Read from left to right: [[math:-7,-2,0,3,5]].

Coldest to warmest: [[math:-7\,^\circ\text{C},-2\,^\circ\text{C},0\,^\circ\text{C},3\,^\circ\text{C},5\,^\circ\text{C}]].

1.2 · Place value and the role of zero

Position gives a digit its value

In a base-ten system, each position is worth ten times the position to its right. The decimal point separates whole-number places from fractional places. A zero can be a placeholder: it keeps the other digits in the correct positions.

{{figure:place-value-chart|The highlighted 4 is in the tens place, so it is worth 40. The zeros in the hundreds and hundredths places are placeholders.}}
Worked example 3

State the value of the 4 in [[math:3{,}048.307]] and calculate [[math:36.2\times 10]].

  1. The 4 is in the tens place, so its value is [[math:40]].
  2. Multiplying by 10 shifts every digit one place left: [[math:36.2\times 10=362]].

The decimal point does not “move” by itself; the digits shift relative to it.

{{exercise:p12:1.2.1,1.2.2,1.2.3,1.2.4}}
1.2 · Place value and the role of zero

Zero, decimals and digit cards

Adding a zero to the right of a whole number multiplies it by 10, but adding a zero after a decimal may leave the value unchanged: [[math:0.4=0.40]]. Multiplying or dividing by powers of ten shifts digits, not just the decimal point.

{{figure:digit-cards|Use each card once. A four-digit number cannot begin with 0.}}
Worked example 4

Use 5, 0, 8 and 2 once each to make the greatest and least four-digit numbers.

  1. Greatest: put the largest digits in the highest places: [[math:8{,}520]].
  2. Least: the first digit cannot be 0, so start with 2, then use the smallest remaining digits: [[math:2{,}058]].

Greatest [[math:8{,}520]]; least [[math:2{,}058]].

1.3 · Expanded form and equivalent representations

One number, several useful forms

Expanded form writes a number as the sum of the values of its digits. It makes the contribution of each place visible. Standard form is the usual compact way of writing the number.

Worked example 5

Write [[math:30.78]] in expanded form, then return to standard form.

  1. Identify each place: 3 tens, 0 ones, 7 tenths, 8 hundredths.
  2. Expanded form: [[math:30+0.7+0.08]].
  3. Adding the parts gives [[math:30.78]] again.

[[math:30.78=30+0.7+0.08=3\times10+7\times0.1+8\times0.01]].

Why zero still matters

[[math:407.3=400+7+0.3]]. The zero tens do not add value, but they keep the 4 in the hundreds place and the 7 in the ones place.

{{exercise:p13:1.3.1,1.3.2,1.3.3,1.3.4}}
1.3 · Expanded form and equivalent representations

Explain the structure, not just the answer

Equivalent representations have the same value but can make different features visible. A place-value chart shows positions; expanded form shows contributions; a number line shows order and distance.

Worked example 6

Explain why [[math:2\times0.1+5\times0.01]] is ten times [[math:2\times0.01+5\times0.001]].

  1. First expression: [[math:0.2+0.05=0.25]].
  2. Second expression: [[math:0.02+0.005=0.025]].
  3. Each digit has moved one place to the left, so each contribution is ten times as large.

[[math:0.25=10\times0.025]].

{{exercise:p13:1.3.5,1.3.6,1.3.7,1.3.8}}
Communicate

When you explain a representation, name the place, state its value, and say what the representation makes easy to see.

1.4 · Comparing, ordering and rounding

Compare place by place

To compare decimals, align the decimal points and compare digits from the highest place downwards. Add trailing zeros if helpful: [[math:0.5=0.50]]. For negative numbers, the number further left on the number line is smaller.

{{figure:decimal-zoom|Many decimals lie between 3.5 and 3.6. The zoomed line shows 3.501 and 3.512.}}
Worked example 7

Order [[math:0.7,0.07,0.77,0.707]] from least to greatest.

  1. Write with three decimal places: [[math:0.700,0.070,0.770,0.707]].
  2. Compare: [[math:0.070<0.700<0.707<0.770]].

[[math:0.07,0.7,0.707,0.77]].

{{exercise:p14:1.4.1,1.4.2}}
Misconception check

“More digits means a larger decimal.” Not always: [[math:6.09<6.1]] because [[math:6.1=6.10]].

1.4 · Comparing, ordering and rounding

Round to a stated place

To round, identify the target place and look at the digit immediately to its right. If that digit is 5 or more, round up; otherwise round down. This book uses the usual convention that a 5 in the deciding place rounds up.

{{figure:rounding-carry|Rounding 39.95 to the nearest tenth gives 40.0 because the hundredths digit is 5 and the carry reaches the whole-number part.}}
Worked example 8

Round [[math:59.96]] to the nearest tenth.

  1. The target is the tenths place: 9. The deciding digit is 6.
  2. Round 9 tenths up to 10 tenths, which is one whole.
  3. The result is [[math:60.0]]. The trailing zero records tenths.

[[math:59.96\approx60.0]] to the nearest tenth.

{{exercise:p14:1.4.3,1.4.4,1.4.5}}
1.4 · Comparing, ordering and rounding

Rounding in context

A rounded number is not the exact value; it is a statement about a range. Always state the place or unit used. In accountability contexts, give the exact figure as well as any rounded headline.

Worked example 9

Give three decimals that round to 6.5 to the nearest tenth, then describe all possible values.

  1. Examples: [[math:6.45,6.50,6.549]].
  2. The lower boundary is 6.45 because the deciding digit is 5.
  3. The upper boundary is just below 6.55, because 6.55 would round to 6.6.

All values from 6.45 inclusive up to but not including 6.55.

{{exercise:p14:1.4.6,1.4.7,1.4.8}}
1.5 · Estimation and sensible accuracy

Estimate first, then check

An estimate uses friendly numbers to predict a result or check whether an exact answer is reasonable. State the rounding you used. When one number is rounded up and another down, the estimate may be high or low; an exact check tells you which.

Worked example 10

Estimate [[math:58\times31]], then calculate exactly.

  1. Round to the nearest ten: [[math:60\times30=1{,}800]].
  2. Exactly: [[math:58\times31=1{,}798]].
  3. The estimate is 2 high.

Estimate 1,800; exact 1,798.

{{exercise:p15:1.5.1,1.5.2,1.5.3,1.5.4}}
Reasoning habit

Ask: “What is this estimate for?” A quick headline, a budget check and an inventory may need different accuracy.

1.5 · Estimation and sensible accuracy

Choose accuracy for a purpose

Extra digits are not automatically more truthful. A calculation can produce many decimal places even when the original measurement was only accurate to the nearest centimetre. Report a level of accuracy that the context can support.

Worked example 11

A library has 1,247,936 books. Suggest a figure for a short public notice.

  1. A headline does not need the exact count.
  2. “About 1,250,000 books” to the nearest ten thousand is readable.
  3. An inventory or audit should use 1,247,936 exactly.

Match the accuracy to the purpose, and state the rounding used.

Common slips

  • Forgetting a placeholder zero: 305 is not 35.
  • Comparing decimals by counting digits.
  • Rounding 39.95 to 39.9 instead of 40.0.
  • Assuming an estimate must be high or low without checking.

Explain how you know

  • Name the place before stating a digit’s value.
  • Use a number line to justify an order.
  • State the target place before rounding.
  • Compare an estimate with an exact result when possible.

Chapter review

What you should now be able to do

Key ideas

  • Number families are nested: natural ⊂ whole ⊂ integer ⊂ rational.
  • Place value gives each digit a value; zero can hold a place.
  • Expanded form shows each digit’s contribution.
  • Compare decimals place by place, not by counting digits.
  • Rounding records a range and a chosen accuracy.
  • Estimates are useful when checked and matched to purpose.
