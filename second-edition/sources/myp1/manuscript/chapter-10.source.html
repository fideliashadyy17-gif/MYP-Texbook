Data, tables and graphs

Data displays and summaries make patterns visible, but choices of grouping, scale and denominator shape the claims that seem reasonable.

  • Describe trends and detect misleading scales or claims.

Chapter 10 · Mathematical development

10.1 Data, variables and tables

Big idea

Identify what was measured, the unit, the population and the type of variable before summarising.

Data are recorded values. A categorical variable names groups (nominal) or ordered categories (ordinal). A numerical variable is discrete if it counts separate values, or continuous if it measures on a scale.

A frequency table lists categories or intervals and their counts. Frequencies must sum to the number of observations. A two-way table shows how two categories overlap.

{{figure:frequency_table-10|A frequency table organises categories; the counts sum to the 200 students surveyed.}}
Worked example · Classify variables

Lunch choice, year group, number of siblings and height.

  1. Lunch choice: nominal categorical.
  2. Year group: ordinal categorical (or discrete numerical code).
  3. Siblings: discrete numerical. Height: continuous numerical.
The variable type guides the display and summary.

10.2 Bar charts and pie charts

Big idea

Use bars to compare categories and sectors to show parts of one whole.

A bar chart uses equal-width bars with heights proportional to frequency. Label axes, start the frequency axis at zero and keep spacing consistent.

A pie chart represents a whole by 360°. Sector angle = category frequency/total × 360°. Percentages should sum to 100% apart from stated rounding.

{{figure:pie_chart-10|Sector angles are proportional to frequency: 172.8°, 133.2° and 54°.}}
Worked example · Lunch choices for 200 students

School 96, packed 74, off-site 30.

  1. School: 96/200×360 = 172.8°.
  2. Packed: 74/200×360 = 133.2°.
  3. Off-site: 30/200×360 = 54°.
Angles sum to 360°.

10.3 Dot plots, histograms and averages

Big idea

Describe shape, centre and spread; choose an average that fits the data and the question.

A dot plot shows individual values. A histogram groups continuous or finely measured numerical data into intervals; with equal widths, bar height is frequency. State the boundary rule.

Mean = sum÷count; median is the middle ordered value (or mean of the two middle values); mode is most frequent; range = maximum−minimum. Outliers pull the mean more than the median.

{{figure:histogram-10|Equal 60-minute intervals. Boundary rule: left endpoint included, right excluded; the final interval includes 360.}}
Worked example · Summarise 30 screen-time values

The ordered data have sum 4,740; the 15th and 16th values are 135 and 140; 120 occurs four times.

  1. Mean = 4740/30 = 158 min.
  2. Median = (135+140)/2 = 137.5 min.
  3. Mode = 120 min; range = 360−45 = 315 min.
Mean 158; median 137.5; mode 120; range 315 minutes.

Chapter 10 · Mathematical development

10.4 Trends, comparisons and misleading graphs

Big idea

Read the scale, denominator and time order before accepting a visual claim.

A line graph or time-ordered bar chart can show change over time. Describe direction, size of change and exceptions; do not call every fluctuation a trend.

Misleading displays include truncated axes, unequal intervals, 3-D distortion, area used for a one-dimensional value, missing denominators and selective time windows. Compare like with like.

{{figure:trend-10|A zero-based axis shows the fall from 88 to 72 and the later recovery to 96 without exaggeration.}}
Worked example · Test “sales doubled”

Sales rose from 50 to 90.

  1. Increase = 40.
  2. Percentage increase = 40/50×100 = 80%.
The claim is false: sales rose by 80%, not 100%.
