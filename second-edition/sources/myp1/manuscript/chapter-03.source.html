Chapter 3 · Mathematical development

3.1 Factors, multiples and factor pairs

Big idea

Search for factor pairs in order, then stop when the partners would repeat.

A factor divides a positive whole number exactly. A multiple is that number times a whole number. Since 7 × 12 = 84, 7 and 12 are factors of 84, and 84 is a multiple of both.

For 36, the pairs are 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9 and 6 × 6. The repeated 6 is counted once. A pair search is complete when the next possible first factor has already appeared as a partner.

{{figure:factors-3|Factor pairs meet at 6 × 6. Search in order so no pair is missed.}}
Worked example · Find every factor of 48

Use factor pairs rather than guessing.

  1. 1 × 48, 2 × 24, 3 × 16, 4 × 12 and 6 × 8.
  2. 5 and 7 do not divide 48. The next partner would repeat.
  3. Collect the factors in order.
1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48: ten factors.

3.2 Divisibility tests

Big idea

Use digit tests as shortcuts, then confirm with an exact multiplication when needed.

Tests for 2, 5 and 10 use the last digit. Tests for 4 and 8 use the last two and three digits because 100 is divisible by 4 and 1,000 by 8.

For 3 and 9, add the digits. A number is divisible by 6 exactly when it is divisible by both 2 and 3. These tests concern positive whole numbers here; a passing test is evidence, not a guess.

Worked example · Test 4,728

Which of 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10 divide it?

  1. Last digit 8: passes 2, fails 5 and 10. Digit sum 21: passes 3, fails 9.
  2. Last two digits 28: passes 4. Last three digits 728 = 8 × 91: passes 8.
  3. Passing 2 and 3 means it also passes 6.
2, 3, 4, 6 and 8.

3.3 Prime and composite numbers

Big idea

Classify by the number of factors, and stop a prime search at the square-root boundary.

A prime is a whole number greater than 1 with exactly two positive factors: 1 and itself. A composite has more than two. The number 1 has one factor, so it is neither.

To test n, try prime divisors no larger than √n. If n = a × b and both factors exceeded √n, their product would exceed n. Thus a composite must have a factor at or below that boundary.

Worked example · Is 97 prime?

Test only the primes up to √97.

  1. √97 is between 9 and 10, so test 2, 3, 5 and 7.
  2. 97 is not divisible by any of them.
97 is prime.

3.4 Prime factorisation

Big idea

Break a composite into factors until every branch ends at a prime.

A factor tree records repeated splitting. Different starting pairs can lead to the same prime factors, apart from order. Index form groups equal primes.

For 84, split 84 into 4 × 21, then 4 into 2 × 2 and 21 into 3 × 7. Thus 84 = 2² × 3 × 7. Multiply the prime product to check the original number.

{{figure:factor_tree-3|Split composite branches until every endpoint is prime: 84 = 2² × 3 × 7.}}
Worked example · Factorise 72

Use a factor tree or repeated division.

  1. 72 = 8 × 9.
  2. 8 = 2³ and 9 = 3².
  3. Check 8 × 9 = 72.
72 = 2³ × 3².

3.5 Highest common factor and lowest common multiple

Big idea

Choose HCF for largest equal groups and LCM for the next shared repeat.

The highest common factor (HCF) is the largest number dividing each given number. The lowest common multiple (LCM) is the smallest positive number that is a multiple of each.

Using prime products, take the smallest exponent of each shared prime for the HCF and the largest exponent of each prime appearing for the LCM. For 12 = 2² × 3 and 18 = 2 × 3², HCF = 6 and LCM = 36.

Worked example · Ribbons and schedules

48 cm and 60 cm ribbons are cut into the longest equal pieces. Two bells repeat every 15 and 20 minutes.

  1. HCF(48,60) = 12, so pieces are 12 cm long.
  2. There are 4 + 5 = 9 pieces.
  3. LCM(15,20) = 60, so the bells next coincide after 60 minutes.
12 cm, 9 pieces; 60 minutes.
