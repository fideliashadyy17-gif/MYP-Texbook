Inquiry statements (verbatim from the audit):

1. The structure of a polynomial — its factors, roots and remainders — determines both what it can be simplified to and what its graph will do.
2. A function is a rule that pairs each input with exactly one output; transforming, composing and inverting are operations on the rule itself, and each leaves a trace on the graph.
3. Exponential change is repeated multiplication; logarithms are the inverse language that turns multiplication into addition and makes the rate visible.
4. A sequence is form made visible: one rule generates every term, and the sum of those terms simplifies to a formula whose behaviour — finite, unbounded or convergent — is decided by a single number.
5. A system of inequalities describes what is possible; a linear objective turns possibility into choice. Solving a quadratic inequality and optimising under linear constraints are the same habit: compare, bound, and justify.
6. A circle conceals equal angles and fixed distances; a few theorems, proved from radii and triangles, let us deduce measurements and justify every claim.
7. Triangles that are not right-angled can still be solved: the sine and cosine rules turn measurements of angle and distance into positions, areas and three-dimensional structure.
8. Vectors encode displacement by magnitude and direction, while matrices encode transformations of the plane as arrays of numbers; the determinant measures how a transformation stretches or collapses area, and decides whether it can be undone.
9. Two groups can share a centre and differ in every other respect; measuring dispersion and association describes those differences precisely, while the origin of the data limits what the description can prove.
10. Probability is a measure on a set of outcomes; conditioning restricts that set, and the difference between P(A ∣ B) and P(B ∣ A) is often the difference between a sound decision and a misleading one.
11. A mathematical model is an argument about the world: its value depends as much on the assumptions it makes and the evidence it ignores as on the accuracy of its arithmetic.
12. Mathematical maturity is the ability to choose a method, justify it, connect it to other ideas, and recognise the limits of what has been shown.
